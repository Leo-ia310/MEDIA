## Synopsis of colorectal cancer: prevalence,


## symptoms, screening, staging, risk factors,

Colorectal cancer (CRC) impacts the colon and rectum, ranking as the fourth deadliest cancer worldwide, accounting
for 10% of cancer cases and 9.4% of cancer-related deaths. CRC is frequently without symptoms until it reaches its
later stages. Screening programs, including colonoscopies and tests on stool samples, have enhanced the chances
of survival for people younger than fifty. The TNM staging system, which evaluates tumor size, lymph node involvement, and metastasis, is commonly utilized. Factors that increase the risk of developing colorectal cancer include
exposure to chemicals, chronic inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, smoking, family history, genetic mutations,
and specific diets. Diets high in fat, consumption of too much iron, sugary beverages, and meals with high sugar
content can elevate the risk of CRC, whereas incorporating whole grains, fiber, calcium, vitamin D, dairy products, vegetables, fruits, and green tea into your diet can lower the risk. Management differs depending on stage: stages 0 and I
are treated with endoscopy and local excision, whereas stages II, III, and IV necessitate systemic treatments like immu-

Screening
CRC screening is advised by the American College of
Gastroenterology for individuals aged 50–75, with the
possibility of initiating at 45. From 1974 to 2013, there
was a 51% rise in CRC cases among people under the
age of 50. By beginning screening at the age of 45, it
is estimated that an additional 25  years of life will be
gained per 1000 individuals [7].
Screening for individuals over the age of 75 should
be personalized according to their risks and life expectancy because they face higher chances of experiencing
negative outcomes such as perforation, false positives,
and dehydration [8].
A screening should be done at 40 years old or 10 years
prior to the youngest affected family member if there
is a first-degree relative with CRC or advanced polyps
before age 60 or two first-degree relatives with CRC
or advanced polyps after age 60. The family history
impacts 10% of people and at least doubles the chances
of developing CRC [9].
Colonoscopy is the primary diagnostic tool for
detecting and treating CRC due to its superior sensitivity compared to other screening methods [10]. Stoolbased tests such as the stool immunochemical test
(FIT), stool DNA test, and high-sensitivity guaiac fecal
occult blood test are all examples that can be used.
Stool DNA tests look for DNA biomarkers linked to
cancer in the cells shed into the stool from the colon
and rectum lining [11].
Currently, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)
has only approved sDNA-FIT as a stool DNA test. This
suggestion is for a stool DNA test that has multiple targets and includes a FIT element. If abnormal results are

found in stool-based testing, a colonoscopy is needed
for additional evaluation [12].
Endoscopic screening in CRC​
Endoscopy screening shows long-term efficacy. However, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy
reports that the quality of the screening is crucial for its
long-term effectiveness in preventing CRC. Colonoscopy is crucial in identifying malignant tumors for future
diagnosis during surgery. Preoperative endoscopic marking could be helpful in identifying flat, small, or inconspicuous colonic lesions that may be difficult to detect
visually or by touch during surgery. There are currently
two options for marking treatments available: inserting
metallic clips or using endoscopic tattooing. India ink
tattoos remain visible for as long as five months after surgery [13, 14].
Colonoscopy and sigmoidoscopy are both endoscopic
screening methods, with the former looking at the entire
colon and the latter focusing on the distal part of the
colon. A colonoscopy is presently the most well-known
endoscopic screening method. The goal of endoscopic
screening is to identify and remove adenomas and serrated polyps to prevent CRC. Furthermore, early cancer detection could be uncovered through endoscopic
screening. Removing polyps reduces the risk of cancer,
while early detection and treatment of cancer also contribute to a decrease in CRC-related deaths. A basic
enema is sufficient for preparing the intestines before the
minimally invasive sigmoidoscopy procedure, which does
not involve anesthesia. Colonoscopy usually requires
conscious sedation or general anesthesia and involves
more time and labor. Colonoscopy is commonly used as

Fecal screening in CRC​
The primary purpose of fecal occult blood testing is for
screening in the early detection of diseases. The reason
for this testing is that blood from early colorectal tumors
can be detected in stools before symptoms appear. Fecal
testing is not highly sensitive due to occasional tumor
leakage. Therefore, a repeat of the fecal testing is necessary [17, 18]. Debates also surround the number of tests
in each round, the recommended starting and ending age
for screening, the ideal interval between fecal screening
cycles, and various other aspects. Positive results from
a fecal test may indicate conditions other than CRC,
including hemorrhoids, peptic ulcers, bleeding polyps,
and inflammation; therefore, a colonoscopy is needed to
verify the results.
Fecal testing can help prevent certain cancers, but its
main benefit lies in detecting cancer early by identifying
and removing polyps during a colonoscopy prompted
by a positive fecal test [19]. In essence, cancer screening

Challenges against effective CRC screening in developing
countries
Difficulties in improving CRC screening among disadvantaged communities involve the following.
Identifying unscreened individuals
The absence of national mechanisms to identify untested
individuals poses a significant challenge, particularly in
developing countries with scarce insurance or regular
healthcare opportunities [21].
Identifying the most effective screening test approach
Various methods of CRC screening differ in terms of
how invasive they are, the amount of time required from
the patient, their level of sensitivity, the infrastructure
needed, and the associated costs. These traits raise questions about a method’s capacity to adequately cater to the
entire population, especially when it comes to marginalized groups. Colonoscopy is frequently advised due to its
higher accuracy in finding cancer and polyps [22].

Cancer is the standard for colorectal disease, relying on

– The extent (size) of the tumor
– The lymph nodes’ involvement: has the cancer spread
to nearby lymph nodes?
– The spread (metastasis) to remote sites: has the cancer spread to distant lymph nodes or distant organs
as the liver or lungs?

### Risk factors

Environmental and genetic factors both play a role in
influencing CRC as shown in Fig. 4; these factors include
the following.

### Exposure to chemicals and carcinogens

Pesticides such as terbufos, dicamba, and acetochlor have
been associated with a higher risk of CRC. Farmers and
rural communities face a high level of exposure [25].

### Chronic inflammatory bowel diseases

Due to changes in gut bacteria, inadequate cellular
response, and increased inflammation affecting cell
growth control, disorders such as Crohn’s disease and
ulcerative colitis can result in CRC [26].

### Obesity

Obesity and excess belly fat are associated with a greater
risk of CRC before the age of 50, as they lead to insulin
resistance, chronic inflammation, and alterations in various hormones that control cell growth [27].

Tobacco smoking
Adenomatous polyps in the colon are precursors of CRC,
and smoking has been associated with a higher likelihood
of their development. Reports suggest that carcinogens
present in cigarette smoke, like aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and nitrosamines, could contribute to cancer formation. Additionally, being exposed
to cigarette smoke has been connected to various genetic
alterations related to the typical mutations seen in colon
and rectal cancer, such as promoter 1A methylation,
tumor suppressor protein p53 (p53) mutation, antigenpresenting cell (APC) mutation, murine sarcoma viral
oncogene homolog B (BRAF) mutation, and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) [28, 29].
Family history
Individuals with a family history of CRC, including parents and siblings, are at higher risk of developing CRC,
especially in younger age groups [30, 31].
Mutations
Genetic factors that play a role in the development of
CRC consist of abnormalities in chromosomes, changes
in epigenetics, and mutations in both somatic and germline cells, such as in genes like RAS, BRAF, and PIK3CA
[32–35].
Types of food
Poor eating habits are associated with a higher likelihood of developing CRC in adults. Consuming high
amounts of fat and calories (especially saturated fat,
Page 5 of 9
monounsaturated fatty acids, and cholesterol) and eating over 100 g of red meat daily are associated with a 98%
increase in the likelihood of developing colorectal adenoma [36–38].
The high-fat diet stimulates gut microorganisms to
produce more secondary bile acids, leading to a cancerpromoting environment with chronic inflammation, high
blood sugar, high insulin levels, and high triglycerides.
High levels of interleukin-6, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, adiponectin, leptin, tumor necrosis factor alpha,
reactive oxygen species overproduction, and C-reactive
protein are strongly associated with hyperinsulinemia,
hyperglycemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. These substances speed up cell growth, disrupt cell communication pathways, weaken the body’s defenses, and prevent
abnormal cells from dying naturally [39, 40]. Several
factors, such as elevated levels of saturated fat, cookinginduced mutagens (e.g., polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, heterocyclic amines, N-nitroso compounds, and
advanced glycation end products), higher intake of heme
iron, and rising insulin resistance, have been associated
with the development of CRC from consuming red or
processed meats [41–44].
Intake of more than 18  mg/day of iron supplements
could raise the likelihood of CRC. Excessive iron can
activate pro-cancer pathways like wingless/integrated
(WNT) signaling, protein kinase (AKT), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), promoting cell survival and cancer
development as well as supporting anti-cancer processes
like oxidative damage and ferroptosis [40, 45, 46].

Diets and beverages high in sugar are positively associated with the risk of CRC. Hyperinsulinemia is caused
by the increase in blood glucose levels after eating sugary foods frequently. Consistently elevated levels of
insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 can increase the
chances of developing cancer by stimulating cell growth
and blocking cell death through internal communication
pathways known as AKT and MAPK [47, 48]. Conversely,
the likelihood of developing CRC decreases with higher
consumption of whole grains and fiber, rather than total
dietary fiber alone. Numerous researchers have discovered that consuming a high amount of fiber increased
the stool volume, reduced colon emptying time, and
enhanced the production of short chain fatty acids and
fermentation, resulting in decreased levels of intestinal
carcinogens [49].

as calcium and vitamin D supplements, can also show
a decrease in the occurrence of CRC. It is believed that
calcium and vitamin D can decrease the occurrence of
colorectal neoplasms through several different methods. Calcium reduces harm to colonic epithelial cells and
promotes their growth and transformation by attaching
to bile acids and free fatty acids. Besides stimulating the

### Conclusion

It is essential to focus on changing controllable risk factors and improving screening efforts in order to tackle
the increasing incidence and death rates of CRC. This
involves working together to decrease obesity, encourage exercise, lessen smoking, and enhance eating habits. Management of CRC requires treatments tailored to
each stage, such as endoscopic and surgical procedures,
preoperative radiation therapy, and different systemic
therapies.
