## I. Introduction

The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and Department of Defense (DOD) Evidence-Based Practice Work Group (EBPWG) was established and first chartered in 2004, with a mission to advise the “…Health Executive Council (HEC) on the use of clinical and epidemiological evidence to improve the health of the population across the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) and Military Health System (MHS),” by facilitating the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for the VA and DOD populations.(1) The development and updating of VA/DOD CPGs is funded by VA Evidence Based Practice, Office of Quality and Patient Safety. The system wide goal of evidence-based guidelines, this CPG, is to guide providers in hypertension management in addition to offering management pathways supported by evidence. This CPG is intended to provide health care providers with a practical framework by which to evaluate, treat, and manage hypertension, address the individual needs and preferences of patients, and improve clinical outcomes. In 2020, the VA and DOD published an updated CPG for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hypertension (HTN) in Primary Care (2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG), which was based on evidence reviewed from December 15, 2013, through March 25, 2019. Since then, a growing body of research has expanded the general knowledge and understanding of the diagnosis and treatment of hypertension. Consequently, a recommendation to update the 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG was initiated in July 2025 using published data from March 1, 2019, through November 12, 2025. This updated CPG’s use of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach reflects a more rigorous application of the methodology than previous iterations.(2) Therefore, the strength of some recommendations might have been modified because of changes in the quality of the supporting evidence (see Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength). This CPG provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and managing adult patients with hypertension who are eligible for care in the VA or DOD systems, including community care. Successful implementation of this CPG will:

- 
Emphasize the use of patient-centered care using risk factors and event history;

- 
Minimize preventable complications and morbidity;

- 
Optimize each patient’s health outcomes and improve quality of life; and

- 
Assess the patient’s condition and in collaboration with the patient, family, and caregivers, determine optimal treatment.

## II. Background

### A. Epidemiology and Impact on the General Population

Blood pressure (BP) is a continuous variable that can increase with advancing age. Elevated blood pressure along with elevated cholesterol, tobacco use, and diabetes, is an established risk factor for conditions like coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cognitive decline. Also, conditions like CKD and/or

Increases in blood pressure have been shown to have a continuous, graded positive association with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (3), and are positively associated with fatal and non-fatal vascular and nonvascular events.(4) Hypertension, or pathologically elevated blood pressure, is a silent asymptomatic condition, which is considered a modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD). CVD has plagued the United States (US) as one of the most common causes of death since the 1900s.(5,6) In 2023, one in every three deaths was associated with CVD.(7) The prevalence of hypertension varies by age, sex, and ethnicity.(8) Blood pressure progressively increases with advancing age; almost half of the elderly population has hypertension, with rates higher in men (50.8%) than women (44.6%).(9) Hypertension develops at a younger age in individuals who are non-Hispanic Black, and may present with a higher prevalence of complications at the time of diagnosis, including stroke and end-stage kidney disease, when compared with other ethnic groups.(10) Jaeger et al. (2023) noted that between 2017 and 2020, a higher percentage of US adults with hypertension compared to those without were non-Hispanic Black (13.5% vs. 9.3%), while a lower percentage of those with hypertension compared to those without were Hispanic (12.3% vs.18.1%).(11) Martin et al. (2024)(8) found that more than half (50.4%) of US males and 43% of females ≥ 20 years of age have hypertension, mirroring the prevalence of CVD, which remains highest among non- Hispanic Black females (59%) and non-Hispanic Black males (58.9%), findings supported by Jaeger et al. (2023).(11) The definition of hypertension has evolved over the past five decades as clinical trials have progressively lowered the level of blood pressure at which the benefits of treatment outweigh the risks. In 2017, the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults redefined hypertension by lowering the threshold levels of 140/90 millimeters of mercury (mmHg) to 130/80 mmHg.(11) Based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which studied 56,017 participants between 2017 and 2020, it is estimated that 115.3 million Americans have hypertension defined by the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline, systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 130 mmHg or higher or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 80 mmHg or higher.(12,13) The asymptomatic nature of hypertension can make detection challenging; therefore, routine screening is paramount. Furthermore, the asymptomatic nature of hypertension can lead to challenges with adherence to treatment. In the US general population, hypertension control rates have decreased from 53.8% controlled (2013-2014) to 43.7% controlled (2017- 2018).(14) It was lowest for Hispanic (36.8%), followed by non-Hispanic Black (38.5%) and non- Hispanic Asian individuals (43.4%). Among US Veterans, hypertension control rates varied across sectors by race and ethnicity (range, 44.1%–97.5%); Black Veterans, followed by American Indian or Alaska Native Veterans, had the lowest mean control rates.(15) There was also significant geographical variance among poorly controlled hypertension control rates.(15) Advances in science and technology have helped drive a decline in mortality; however, clinicians still need to evolve their practice and remain engaged with their patients as they continue to help improve modifiable risk factors in their patients. In this CPG, the Work Group specifically investigated the point of proven benefit for hypertension treatment (see Recommendations 4-6); based on this evidence, the Work Group defines hypertension as SBP and/or DBP at or above 130/90 mmHg, assuming proper measurement technique.

### B. Hypertension in the Department of Veterans Affairs and Department

### of Defense Populations

Estimates of HTN prevalence among active-duty military are limited and undoubtedly skewed by policies that limit recruitment of individuals who already have cardiovascular risk factors such as HTN, obesity, and diabetes. Survey data report an overall prevalence of 9.1% in the active-duty population, with higher rates in men and more senior pay grades.(16) Compared to active-duty military who have not deployed, exposure to multiple stressful deployments was associated with a new diagnosis of HTN.(17) In the first study to directly compare cardiovascular health metrics of active-duty Army men and women with those of NHANES participants evaluated between 2011 and 2012, NHANES participants had a higher prevalence of ideal blood pressure defined as <120/80 mmHg (46% versus 27% for men, 63% versus 52% for women) compared to active-duty Army personnel, even though NHANES participants were considerably older than the Army personnel (only 50% of NHANES participants <40 years old).(18) Applying the AHA and ACC definition of HTN as a systolic blood pressure of ≥130 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg (11), the prevalence of HTN among Veterans was 48.2% in fiscal years 2016-2019.(19) This prevalence closely aligns with the 48.1% prevalence reported for the US general population, based on NHANES data from 2017 to 2020.(20) Similar to the general population, the prevalence of HTN among Veterans increases with age, ranging from 10.7% among individuals 18-44 years to 62.7% among those aged 65 years and older. Male Veterans had a higher prevalence of HTN (50.1%) compared to female Veterans (27.3%). Among racial and ethnic groups, the prevalence was highest among Black Veterans (54.2%), followed by White (49.5%), Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islanders (47.4%), Hispanic (39.6%), and Asian (36.5%).(21) Blood pressure control rates, defined by a treatment goal of <140/90 mmHg among patients aged 45-64, were 76.6% for female and 75% for male Veterans in fiscal years 2016- 2019. These control rates are higher than those reported in the general population (40.5%).(20) However, among all Veterans, younger Veterans and those with diabetes or residing in rural areas had lower rates of blood pressure control.(19) This CPG on the management of HTN in the primary care setting is intended to promote evidence-based management of HTN and thereby improve patients’ clinical outcomes. It can assist primary care providers in the screening and diagnosis of HTN, determination of appropriate treatment, and delivery of individualized interventions. Although it was developed for a broad range of clinical settings, it should be applied with enough flexibility to accommodate local practice and individual situations.

### C. Hypertension Impact on Readiness in the Military

Hypertension poses a direct threat to military readiness, impairing Service members’ health, performance, and overall operational readiness, as well as increasing risk for cardiovascular events. Hypertension is common in the DOD, with survey data from 2018 reporting that 9.1% of the active-duty population carried a hypertension diagnosis.(16) Evidence suggests that hypertension is underdiagnosed and undertreated in the military population, potentially increasing the risk of short-term impact on readiness and long-term adverse cardiovascular consequences.(18,22) Risk factors for developing hypertension, such as obstructive sleep apnea (23), tobacco use (16), and non-regulated supplement use (24) may be more common in the active-duty population compared to the general population in the United States. Furthermore,

August 2026 Page 8 of 123 common military exposures, such as combat, sleep deprivation, and traumatic experiences resulting in chronic post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) all increase the risk of hypertension.(17,25-27) The proper diagnosis of hypertension is critical in the active-duty population. Consideration of white coat effect and white coat hypertension are important, as a new diagnosis could adversely impact duty status and unit readiness, while proper measurement of blood pressure may be challenging in operational clinical environments. Improper measurement can lead to both over and underdiagnosis. The immediate impact on readiness for Service members with hypertension depends on many factors, such as the degree of blood pressure elevation, the Service member’s occupation or special duty, the timeline and location of the deployment, and the availability of medical resources while deployed. The diagnosis of hypertension is not immediately disqualifying for most duties, but certain special communities, such as aviation, have specific requirements for the assessment, management, and control of hypertension. In general, a Service member with significantly elevated blood pressure is unlikely to be fully medically ready or world-wide deployable, regardless of occupation. Mild elevations in blood pressure will restrict Service members in special populations from achieving full medical readiness. One study (28) found a six-fold increase in the prevalence of hypertension in Naval aviators with application of the more stringent hypertension diagnostic criteria from the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines (29) compared to the Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC7) Guidelines, with up to 45% of Naval aviators potentially having the diagnosis of hypertension. Blood pressure control is seldom achieved rapidly as titration of therapy over months is often required, which may restrict a Service member from full readiness and deployability for an extended period. Furthermore, some medications may be restricted or limited in special populations (such as beta blockers in aviation). Other first-line medications may be challenging to initiate and titrate in austereenvironments (such as diuretics in heat environments), when routine laboratory monitoring is not available, or when side effects of therapy could jeopardize the Service member’s health and adversely affect the mission. Most active-duty Service members can be fully medically ready and deployable world-wide with the diagnosis of hypertension if the diagnosis is made and management is initiated and managed in accordance with this CPG. In addition to the long-term health benefits, early and optimal treatment of hypertension can ensure blood pressure does not impact Service members’ readiness.

## III. Scope of This Guideline

This CPG is based on published clinical evidence and related information available between March 1, 2019, and November 12, 2025. It is intended to provide general guidance on best evidence-based practices (see Appendix A for additional information on the evidence review methodology). Although the CPG is intended to improve the quality of care and clinical outcomes (see Introduction), it is not intended to define a standard of care (i.e., mandated or strictly required care).

### A. Evidence Quality and Recommendation Strength

The Work Group used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to craft each recommendation and determine its strength. Per the GRADE approach, recommendations must be evidence-based and cannot be made based on expert opinion alone. The GRADE approach uses the following four domains to inform the strength of each recommendation (see Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction)(32): 1. Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes 2. Confidence in the quality of the evidence 3. Patient or provider values and preferences 4. Other implications, as appropriate (e.g., resource use, equity, acceptability, feasibility, subgroup considerations) Using these four domains, the Work Group determined the relative strength of each recommendation (Strong or Weak). ### A. Patient-Centered Care

VA and DOD encourage providers to be sensitive to demographic, cultural, and other differences that affect patients’ values, needs, and preferences. Patient-centered care is aimed at treating the condition while also optimizing the individual’s overall health and well-being. Regardless of the care setting, all patients should have access to individualized evidence-based care. Patient- centered care can decrease patient anxiety, increase trust in providers, and improve treatment adherence.(40,41)A holistic health approach (https:/www.va.gov/wholehealth/) empowers and equips individuals to meet their personal health and well-being goals. Good communication is essential and should be supported by evidence-based information tailored to each patient’s needs. Guideline recommendations should be applied in a holistic approach to care that is patient- centered, culturally appropriate, and available to people with limited literacy skills and physical, sensory, or learning disabilities. The focus is using an individual’s risk factors and event history to guide the various treatment and management strategies for patients with hypertension.

### B. Shared Decision-Making

This CPG encourages providers to practice shared decision making (SDM), a process in which providers, patients, and patient care partners (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) consider clinical evidence of benefits and risks as well as patient values and preferences to make decisions regarding the patient’s treatment.(42) SDM is emphasized in “Crossing the Quality Chasm”, an Institute of Medicine, now NAM, report in 2001 (43) and is a core component of a patient- centered, whole health approach. Providers must be adept at presenting information to their patients regarding individual treatments, expected risks, possible outcomes, and levels and/or settings of care, especially where patient heterogeneity in weighing risks and benefits might exist. The VA and DOD have embraced SDM. Providers are encouraged to use SDM to individualize treatment goals and plans based on patient capabilities, needs, values, and preferences.

### Discussion

The diagnosis and management of HTN has been based primarily on measurement of blood pressure in the primary care office setting. Office measurements were used by most observational studies and all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to define both the risk of various blood pressure levels and the goal blood pressures that lead to reduction in adverse cardiovascular outcomes or mortality. Historically, office blood pressure was measured manually using a mercury sphygmomanometer. However, manual office blood pressure (MOBP) measurements are no longer recommended due to human observer bias, calibration issues, and falsely elevated 'white coat' readings. Instead, recent studies have used fully automated oscillometric sphygmomanometers (automatic office blood pressure [AOBP]), programmed to have a five- minute wait time followed by two to three BP measurements separated by 30-60 seconds and report averaged results. During the 2020 CPG recommendation development, two SRs by Pappaccogli et al. (2019)(57) and Roerecke et al. (2019)(58) indicated that in-office blood pressure readings mimicking the technique used in the clinical studies were very similar to daytime average ambulatory blood pressure monitors (ABPM). Routine office readings, commonly performed as a single attended measurement (OBPM), were significantly higher than measurements from fully automated and averaged readings. A study by Bacaicoa et al. (2025)(59) again confirmed AOBP (waiting room or clinic room) had better diagnostic accuracy than office blood pressure measurement (OBPM). Other commonly performed methods of BP measurement include home blood pressure measurement (HBPM), kiosk blood pressure measurement, and ABPM to include 12-hour and 24-hour monitors. The 2025 evidence review assessed the most accurate method for measuring blood pressure, resulting in the inclusion of four SRs and 17 individual studies. Apart from two studies, all compared a measurement method (HBPM, AOBP, MOBP, attended or unattended OBPM, waiting room blood pressure, kiosk blood pressure, and one-hour clinic AOBP) against ABPM as the reference standard. The reference standard in each study varied, with different thresholds for the diagnosis of HTN and different methods and duration for obtaining ABPM. In addition, some studies evaluated methods to establish the diagnosis of HTN, while other studies evaluated methods for monitoring therapy. An SR/meta-analysis (MA) by Karnjanapiboonwong et al.

### Recommendation

3. We suggest offering home blood pressure self-monitoring with co-interventions (e.g., nurse or pharmacist-led educational interventions; or the use of various mobile apps) to improve adherence to therapy. (Weak for | Reviewed, Amended)

### Discussion

Home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) should utilize an automated blood-pressure cuff (either purchased by the patient or provided by the health-care system) to measure blood pressure at home so that trends of out-of-office blood pressure can be followed. Our evidence review included five SRs and 11 RCTs, which found greater adherence to therapy by patients who utilized HBPM both in isolation and in conjunction with a co-intervention. Co-interventions, such as nurse- or pharmacist-led education, or the use of various mobile apps, appear to more consistently improve adherence to therapy than HBPM alone. Both the 2014 and 2020 VA/DOD Hypertension CPGs recommend using HBPM for both diagnosis and treatment. Notably, Recommendation 5 within the 2020 update specifically notes that HBPM alone has a limited effect, but when paired with co-interventions, it significantly improves blood pressure control. The 2020 CPG also noted that HBPM, including tailored medication titration and education or lifestyle counseling, was associated with a lower blood pressure (MD [mean difference] in SBP - 6.1 mmHg). The current recommendation focuses only on the treatment of hypertension, specifically adherence to therapy, and achievement of blood pressure goals as the outcome. The current recommendation is based on very low-quality evidence from an updated literature review. Evidence for the use of HBPM without co-interventions was mixed. ### Discussion

A meta-analysis that included eight randomized (or quasi-randomized) controlled trials consisting of 2,444 participants was reviewed. This study included individuals greater than 18 years old, with hypertension (diastolic BP >95 mmHg and significant atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis [unilateral or bilateral reduction in luminal diameter of >50%]). Patients with fibromuscular dysplasia, total occlusion of the renal artery, an affected kidney size of less than eight centimeters, malignant hypertension, or a serum creatinine >500 mol/L (>5.66 mg/dL) were excluded. The trials compared either balloon angioplasty (with or without stent insertion) to medical therapy. Overall results demonstrated that intervention with angioplasty/stent was not associated with an improvement in SBP (-1.1, 95% CI, 3.4 – 1.2 mmHg); cardiovascular outcomes (OR [odds ratio] 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7 – 1.1]), renal adverse outcomes (OR 0.9 [95% CI, 0.7 – 1.3]), or serum creatinine at 2 years (MD -6.5 µmol/L [95% CI, -19.5 to 6.5µmol/L)(MD -0.07 mg/dL [95% CI, -0.2 to 0.07 mg/dL]). ### Research Priorities

During the development of the 2026 VA/DOD Hypertension CPG, the Work Group identified topics needing additional research, including areas requiring stronger evidence to support current recommendations and research exploring new areas to guide future CPG updates.

### A. Monitoring

Further research evaluating clinical outcomes, specifically cardiovascular outcomes, based on treatment to home blood pressure goals, is needed to support HBPM. Evaluation of the cost- benefit for providing HBPM to Veterans and active-duty Service members, with a specific focus on the potential long-term cost vs. benefit of better blood pressure control throughout the entire health-care enterprise, should be explored. Studies should also be done to assess barriers on the implementation of AOBP, as adoption has been limited.

### B. Blood Pressure Goals

Future research should prioritize identifying the most promising population(s) for more intensive SBP lowering and whether even lower SBP targets (<120 mmHg) are more beneficial as compared with a SBP goal of <130 mmHg. Additionally, research should consider age specifics for goals given the large age span across the active-duty DOD to VA patient population, considering those who are elderly, frail, and possibly unable to tolerate a more intensive target. Further clinical trials should also explore exclusively enrolling individuals with an elevated DBP > 90mmHg.

### C. Pharmacotherapy

Future studies should explore outcome data in those with resistant hypertension, as they are at increased risk for cardiovascular events, to inform clinicians on which fourth-line drug will have the greatest impact on cardiovascular events. Research should also be conducted on the impact of age, sex, and comorbidities like diabetes, CKD, coronary artery disease (CAD), or prior stroke on the response to specific medications in patients with resistant hypertension. Head-to-head comparisons of alternative agents are needed, including aprocitentan and baxdrostat, as well as RCTs that evaluate the safety and efficacy of stepped therapy versus multidrug combination single-pill medication using FDA-approved medications in VA/DOD or similar populations. Additionally, robust head-to-head comparisons evaluating within-medication- class differences, including differences in age, hypertension stage, comorbidities, morbidity, and mortality, and pharmacogenetics to aid in identifying responders, would be beneficial.

### D. Lifestyle Interventions

Future research in this space should stratify the effects of physical activity on BP by frequency, intensity, and duration of exercise, including the use of cardiac rehabilitation or other group exercise classes provided by trained personnel. Socioeconomic status of the sample population should be explored to provide more insight into equity, feasibility, and subgroup considerations. Additionally, research on smoking and alcohol cessation and how it impacts blood pressure should be done using bio-verification vs. self-reporting.

### E. Other Treatment Modalities

Future research should explore healthcare professional-led interventions to manage blood pressure. Studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of pharmacist-led interventions for hypertension, given their resource-use implications. Dietitian-led interventions should also be evaluated, focusing on the intensity and duration of the intervention types that are most effective for improving blood pressure control. Additionally, more research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions to manage hypertension across specific subgroups (e.g., age, ethnicity, comorbidities) and to explore the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions that include initiation and titration of antihypertensive medications using established protocols. Further research is needed to explore long-term comparisons between incretin-based therapies and bariatric surgery, as well as adherence differences with various weight loss medications, to include GLP-1 receptor agonists, and assess the long-term effects of these medications and the sustainability of desired outcomes in patients. Additionally, some populations remain unstudied regarding renal artery stenosis (RAS), including those with fibromuscular dysplasia or those with recurrent hospitalizations for volume issues; these should be objectives for future research. In these patients, it may be reasonable to attempt intervention for RAS or involve them in future clinical trials. Future research should also evaluate the long-term effects of renal artery denervation on blood pressure, as well as its impact on clinical outcomes. Lastly, future research should include continued research with new and emerging digital health technologies to include artificial intelligence integration/assistance, synchronous versus asynchronous modalities, and explore demographic preferences in relation to digital health technology uptake.

### a. General Criteria for Inclusion in Systematic Evidence Review

- 
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or SRs of RCTs published on or after March 1, 2019, to November 12, 2025.

- 
Studies must have been published in English.

- 
Publication must have been a full clinical study or SR; abstracts alone were not included. Similarly, letters, editorials, research protocols, and other publications that were not full- length clinical studies were not accepted as evidence.

- 
Systematic reviews must have searched MEDLINE or EMBASE for eligible publications, performed a risk of bias assessment of included studies, and assessed the quality of evidence using a recognizable rating system, such as GRADE or something compatible (e.g., the one used by the AHRQ Evidence-based Practice Centers [EPCs]). If an existing review did not assess the overall quality of the evidence, evidence from the review was reported in a manner that allowed us to judge the overall risk of bias, consistency, directness, and precision of evidence. We did not use an existing review as evidence if we were not able to assess the overall quality of the evidence in the review. If multiple SRs addressed a KQ, we selected the most recent and/or comprehensive review. In many cases, we used multiple SRs to address a single KQ, as each SR dealt with a different intervention or intervention-comparator combination.

- 
RCTs must have had an independent control group. Randomized crossover trials were only included if data from the first period (prior to treatment crossover) was reported separately or an adequate washout period was used.

- 
For KQ 1, diagnostic accuracy studies and SRs of diagnostic accuracy were included. For KQ 3 (weight loss), RCTs with pre-planned subgroup analyses, observational studies and SRs of observational studies that report on groups who did and did not lose weight were used to address the KQ. For all other KQs, only SRs of RCTs and individual RCTs were considered.

- 
Study must have enrolled at least 20 patients (10 per study group for RCTs and 20 for observational studies).

- 
Study must have enrolled at least 85% of patients who meet the study population criteria. If the patient population fell below this threshold but the relevant population of patients was reported separately, then that study was included.

- 
To ensure applicability to the VA/DOD healthcare systems, and ensure consistency across the CPG program, inclusion of individual studies was limited to very high Human Development Index (HDI), countries with an index ≥0.8 where standards of healthcare are comparable (e.g., United States, Canada, United Kingdom, Western Europe, Israel, Japan, Hong Kong, Australia, and New Zealand). Inclusion of the systematic reviews was limited to those including more than half of the studies from eligible regions. o These regions of interest are listed in Table 1 of the Statistical Annex of the 2023/24 Human Development Report produced by the United Nations Development Program.

Study must have reported on at least one outcome of interest.

### c. Rating the Quality of Individual Studies and the Body of Evidence

Sigma Health Consulting assessed the methodological risk of bias of individual diagnostic, observational, and interventional studies using the USPSTF method. Each study is assigned a rating of Good, Fair, or Poor based on a set of criteria that vary depending on study design. Detailed lists of criteria and definitions appear in Appendix VI of the USPSTF procedure manual.(36) Next, Sigma Health Consulting assessed the overall quality of the body of evidence for each critical and important outcome using the GRADE approach. This approach considers the following factors: overall study quality (or overall risk of bias or study limitations), consistency of evidence, directness of evidence, and precision of evidence. The overall quality of the body of evidence is rated as High, Moderate, Low, and Very Low.

### a. Determining Recommendation Strength and Direction

Per GRADE methodology, to assess the quality of the evidence base and assign a grade for the strength for each recommendation, the GRADE system uses the following four domains to assess the strength of each recommendation (32): 1. Confidence in the Quality of the Evidence Confidence in the quality of theevidence reflects the quality of the evidence base and the certainty in that evidence. This second domain reflects the methodological quality of the studies for each outcome variable. In general, the strength of recommendation follows the level of evidence, but not always, as other domains may increase or decrease their strength. The evidence review used for the development of recommendations for hypertension diagnosis and management, conducted by Sigma Health Consulting, assessed the confidence in the quality of the evidence base and assigned a rate of “High,” “Moderate,” “Low,” or “Very low.” The elements that go into the confidence in the quality of the evidence include:

- 
Is there high or moderate quality evidence that answers this question?

- 
What is the overall certainty of this evidence? 2. Balance of Desirable and Undesirable Outcomes Balance of desirable and undesirable outcomes refers to the size of anticipated benefits (e.g., increased longevity, reduction in morbid event, resolution of symptoms, improved quality of life, decreased resource use) and harms (e.g., decreased longevity, immediate serious complications, adverse event, impaired quality of life, increased resource use, inconvenience/hassle) relative to each other. This domain is based on the understanding that most providers will offer patients therapeutic or preventive measures if the advantages of the intervention exceed the risks and adverse effects. The certainty or uncertainty of the provider about the risk-benefit balance will greatly influence the strength of the recommendation. Some of the discussion questions that fall under this domain include:

- 
Given the best estimate of typical values and preferences, are you confident that the benefits outweigh the harms and burden or vice versa?

- 
Are the desirable anticipated effects large?

- 
Are the undesirable anticipated effects small?

Patient Values and Preferences “Patient values and preferences” is an overarching term that includes patients’ perspectives, beliefs, expectations, and goals for health and life. More precisely, it refers to the processes that individuals use in considering the potential benefits, harms, costs, limitations, and inconvenience of the therapeutic or preventive measures in relation to one another. For some, the term “values” has the closest connotation to these processes. For others, the connotation of “preferences” best captures the notion of choice. In a situation in which the balance of benefits and risks are uncertain, eliciting the values and preferences of patients and empowering them and their surrogates to make decisions consistent with their goals of care becomes even more important. ### Appendix E: Patient Focus Group Methods and Findings

### A. Methods

VA and DOD Leadership recruited seven participants for the focus group with support from the Champions and other Work Group members as needed. The goal of recruitment for this Patient Focus Group was to have a group of engaging, diverse patients receiving VA or DOD healthcare services, who could cogently explain their experience with hypertension. Participants were mixed in terms of receiving care from the VA and DOD, with one receiving care from some civilian providers. Participants’ length of hypertension treatment ranged widely from as recent as two years to as long as 30 years. The Work Group, with support from Sigma Health Consulting, identified topics on which participants’ input was important to consider in developing the CPG. Sigma Health Consulting developed, and the Work Group approved, an interview guide covering these topics. The focus group facilitator who led the discussion used the guide to elicit the participants’ perspectives about their treatment and overall care. Not all questions included in the Moderator’s Guide for the Hypertension Patient Focus Group were addressed by participants, but because the moderator encouraged conversation between the Patient Focus Group members, most topics were covered.

### B. Patient Focus Group Findings

### a. Participants stressed the importance of whole-person approaches using

### individualized treatment plans

- 
Most participants emphasized that treatments should be individualized because a ‘one- size-fits-all' approach often fails to address high blood pressure.

- 
Many participants mentioned that they wanted to resist ‘lifelong pills’ and instead focus on lifestyle changes like quitting smoking, losing weight, and adjusting their diet.

### b. Participants emphasized that continuity of providers, integrated records, care

### coordination, and communication between VA and DOD systems are essential

### for effective treatment and engagement.

- 
Some participants reported that they received care from providers at different facilities, and the communication between providers was lacking, causing discrepancies and the participants feeling as though they had to relay information.

- 
Overall, participants emphasized the need for stronger communication and coordination amongst all providers (primary care, specialists, pharmacists, etc.)

### c. Participants emphasized the importance of accurate screening and early

### diagnosis of high blood pressure, hyperaldosteronism, and co-occurring

### conditions.

- 
Participants noted that early diagnosis and treatment of hypertension and co-occurring conditions would help patients reduce their cardiovascular risk and get their hypertension and the side effects of medications under control sooner.
